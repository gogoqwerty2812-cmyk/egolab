How to read a supplement lab report (certificate of analysis)

12 Nov 2026 · 2 min read · EgoLab

A lab report is the only way to know what is really in a supplement — beyond what the marketing says. They look intimidating, but you only need to check a handful of things. Here is a walkthrough.

What a certificate of analysis is

A certificate of analysis (COA) is a document from a laboratory showing the test results for one specific production batch. It usually answers three questions: is the active ingredient there in the stated amount, are contaminants below safe limits, and is the product free of microbes and, sometimes, banned substances.

Step 1: check who did the test

Look for the laboratory's name, address and accreditation. An independent lab accredited to ISO/IEC 17025 is the gold standard. A report from the manufacturer's own quality department ("in-house") is better than nothing, but a third-party result carries far more weight.

Step 2: match the batch

Find the batch or lot number on the report and compare it with the number printed on your tub, usually near the expiry date. They should match. Also check the test date — it should be close to when that batch was made, not years earlier.

Step 3: read the results that matter

  • Active content. For protein, the measured protein per 100 g should be close to the label. For creatine, look for purity (typically 99%+). Some variance is normal; large gaps are not.
  • Heavy metals. Lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), arsenic (As) and mercury (Hg), reported in ppm (mg/kg) or µg per serving. Each should be below the stated limit, often shown as "< LOQ" — below the level the lab can measure.
  • Microbiology. Total plate count, yeast and mould, and absence of pathogens such as Salmonella and E. coli.
  • Banned substances. Competitive athletes should look for screening under programmes like Informed Sport or similar, which test for substances on anti-doping lists.

Red flags

  • No laboratory name, or a lab you cannot find online.
  • No batch number, or a batch that does not match your product.
  • Cropped, blurry or heavily edited images with key parts missing.
  • Results that are suspiciously round or identical across different batches.
  • A single old report reused for years of production.

The short version

  1. Independent, ideally ISO/IEC 17025 accredited lab.
  2. Batch number matches your tub.
  3. Active content close to the label.
  4. Heavy metals and microbes below the limits.
  5. No signs of editing or recycled reports.

Frequently asked questions

What is a certificate of analysis for supplements?

A certificate of analysis (COA) is a lab document showing test results for one specific production batch: what is inside, how much, and whether contaminants are below safety limits.

How do I know a lab report belongs to my product?

The batch or lot number on the report must match the number printed on your tub, and the test date should be close to the production date.

What is the difference between an in-house and a third-party test?

An in-house test is done by the manufacturer itself. A third-party test is done by an independent lab, ideally accredited to ISO/IEC 17025, which makes the result far more trustworthy.

Which heavy metals should a supplement be tested for?

Lead, cadmium, arsenic and mercury. Results are usually reported in ppm or µg per serving and should be below the relevant legal limits.
