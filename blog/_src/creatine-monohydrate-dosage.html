Creatine is the most researched sports supplement there is, and also one of the cheapest. It reliably helps with short, hard efforts — heavy sets, sprints, repeated jumps — and the instructions are simple. Here is what actually matters.

What creatine does

Your muscles use a molecule called phosphocreatine to regenerate ATP, the fuel for the first few seconds of an all-out effort. Supplementing creatine raises the amount stored in muscle by roughly 20–40%. In practice that means one or two more reps at the same weight, slightly faster recovery between sets, and, over months, more total training work — which is where the extra strength and muscle come from.

It is not a stimulant. You will not "feel" a dose the way you feel caffeine; the effect builds up as your muscle stores fill.

How much to take

The standard dose is 3–5 g of creatine monohydrate per day. A level teaspoon is about 3–5 g, but a scoop or a cheap kitchen scale is more accurate. Heavier athletes sometimes take up to around 0.1 g per kg of body weight, but for most people 5 g is plenty.

Loading phase: optional

Loading means taking about 20 g a day — four doses of 5 g — for 5–7 days, then dropping to 3–5 g. It saturates your muscles within a week instead of three to four. The end result is the same, and loading is more likely to upset your stomach. If you are not in a hurry, skip it.

When to take it

Daily consistency beats perfect timing. Some studies hint at a small advantage for taking it around training, but the difference is minor. Pick a moment you will not forget: with breakfast, or in your post-workout protein shake. On rest days, take it anyway.

Creatine mixes best in warm liquid and does not need to be "cycled" — there is no evidence that taking breaks improves results.

Water weight and other side effects

A gain of 1–2 kg during the first weeks is normal. It is water stored inside the muscle cells along with the creatine, not fat and not the puffy, under-the-skin kind of bloating. Large single doses can cause stomach discomfort; splitting the dose or taking it with food helps.

In healthy people, decades of studies at recommended doses have not shown harm to the kidneys or liver. Creatine can slightly raise creatinine on a blood test, which is a lab marker, not a sign of kidney damage — but tell your doctor you take it. If you have kidney disease or take medication that affects the kidneys, talk to a doctor first.

Which creatine to buy

  • Creatine monohydrate — the form used in almost all research. "Micronized" monohydrate is the same thing ground finer, so it dissolves more easily.
  • HCl, ethyl ester, buffered creatine — usually more expensive, and none has been shown to work better than monohydrate.
  • Gummies and "creatine drinks" — check the label. Some have been found to contain far less creatine than claimed.

Look for a product that lists only creatine monohydrate, states the grams per serving clearly, and has a third-party lab report for the batch you are buying. See our guide on how to read a supplement lab report.

The short version

  • 3–5 g of creatine monohydrate every day.
  • Loading is optional; it only gets you there faster.
  • Timing barely matters — consistency does.
  • 1–2 kg of water weight early on is normal.
  • Plain monohydrate with a lab report beats fancy forms.
